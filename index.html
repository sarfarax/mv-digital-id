An identity card you can check without asking anyone

The card carries the same details as the front of a Maldivian ID card. Its QR code holds those details plus a perceptual hash of the portrait, all signed with the issuing authority’s BLS12-381 key. A verifier reads the code offline, confirms the signature, then re-photographs the portrait and checks it still hashes to the signed value.

What the QR code contains

Everything before the signature is what gets signed:

MV1#A123456#Aishath Nasheeda Ibrahim#F#1991-04-17#+960 771 2345#Ma. Blue Heaven, Male, Maldives#30b2e6c3d32d0bf4#<signature>

The trailing 16 hex characters before the signature are the portrait’s 64-bit perceptual hash. That single field is what ties the photograph to the data: alter the picture and the hash no longer matches, alter the hash and the signature breaks.

The whole string is compressed with LZString and drawn as a QR code, which lands around 276 bytes — a version 11 symbol.

How verification works

  1. The verifier finds the QR code in a camera frame and decompresses the payload.
  2. The BLS signature is checked against the issuer’s published public key. If it fails, nothing else matters.
  3. jsQR reports the QR’s four corners. Because the card is a rigid, fixed layout, those corners determine where the portrait must be.
  4. The portrait is lifted out of the frame with perspective and rotation removed, then normalised to the same 300×400 grayscale raster the issuer hashed.
  5. Its perceptual hash is compared with the signed one. A handful of differing bits is ordinary camera noise; a large difference means a different face.

What this does not do

This is a working demonstration, not a deployable identity system. The gaps are deliberate and worth stating plainly:

  • The demo signing key is committed to this repository, so anyone can mint a card that this scanner accepts. A real issuer keeps that key in hardware.
  • There is no revocation, no expiry date, and no way to tell a reported-stolen card from a valid one. A signature says only “this was issued”, never “this is still good”.
  • The portrait check compares a photograph to a photograph. It confirms the picture on the card is the signed one; it does not confirm the person holding the card is the person in the picture. That is a job for a human or for face recognition, neither of which is here.
  • Nothing in the payload is encrypted. Anyone who scans the card can read every field, exactly as they could by looking at it.

Built on

The signing approach follows oelna/signed-qr-codes, which demonstrates BLS-signed QR payloads; its Noble BLS, LZString and Nayuki QR modules are vendored unchanged. Reading QR codes uses jsQR. The image hash is a JavaScript port of the DCT hash from pHash.org.

No build step and no backend: every page is static and every check runs in the browser.
