An identity card you can check without asking anyone

The card carries the same details as the front of a Maldivian ID card. Its QR code holds those details plus a perceptual hash of the portrait, all signed with the issuing authority’s BLS12-381 key. A verifier reads the code offline, confirms the signature, then re-photographs the portrait and checks it still hashes to the signed value.

What the QR code contains

Everything before the signature is what gets signed:

MV2#A123456#Aishath Nasheeda Ibrahim#F#1991-04-17#2036-08-27#Ma. Blue Heaven, Male, Maldives#290d5eb82deaf087#<signature>

The trailing 16 hex characters before the signature are the portrait’s 64-bit perceptual hash. That single field is what ties the photograph to the data: alter the picture and the hash no longer matches, alter the hash and the signature breaks.

The whole string is compressed with LZString and drawn as a QR code, which lands around 270 bytes — a version 10 symbol. An Enhanced card adds a 32-character face code and needs version 11.

Two assurance levels

Standard (MV2): signature, expiry, and a perceptual hash binding the printed portrait. Proves the card and its photograph are genuine.

Enhanced (MV3): everything above, plus a signed 128-bit face code. The verifier photographs the person presenting the card, reduces their face to the same code on the device, and compares the two bit by bit — so a genuine card in the wrong hands is caught. Calibrated on a public face dataset, the default threshold accepts 0.1% of different people; on the bundled Maldivian sample portraits the off-the-shelf model separates faces less well, so it needs measuring on real enrolment photos before anyone relies on it.

Both levels are live in this demo. The face models (about 7 MB) download only when an Enhanced card is checked; Standard cards never touch them. See the proposal for the design and its limits.

Why a signed payload, not a link?

A URL QR is easy to print and easy to fake. A forger points at a site that looks official and shows a green tick — no cryptography required. Clone domains (verify-dnr.gov.mv, dnr.gov-mv.com, homographs) are routine phishing, and spotting the wrong URL under time pressure is not a reliable control.

This design puts the evidence in the QR itself: a BLS12-381 signature checked against a pinned public key, with no network call. A convincing website cannot forge that signature. Online status checks can still sit beside it for revocation, but they do not replace cryptographic verification.

How verification works

  1. The verifier finds the QR code in a camera frame and decompresses the payload.
  2. The BLS signature is checked against the issuer’s published public key. If it fails, nothing else matters.
  3. The expiry date is read from the signed payload and compared with today. It is inside the signature, so it cannot be edited to extend a card’s life.
  4. jsQR reports the QR’s four corners. Because the card is a rigid, fixed layout, those corners determine where the portrait must be.
  5. The portrait is lifted out of the frame with perspective and rotation removed, then normalised to the same 300×400 grayscale raster the issuer hashed.
  6. Its perceptual hash is compared with the signed one. A handful of differing bits is ordinary camera noise; a large difference means a different face.
  7. On an Enhanced card, the verifier also captures the person presenting it, computes their face code locally, and compares it with the signed code.

What this does not do

This is a working demonstration, not a deployable identity system. The gaps are deliberate and worth stating plainly:

  • The demo signing key is committed to this repository, so anyone can mint a card that this scanner accepts. A real issuer keeps that key in hardware.
  • The expiry date is checked offline, but there is no revocation: a card reported stolen the day after issue still verifies. A signature says only “this was issued”, never “this is still good”.
  • On a Standard card, the portrait check compares a photograph to a photograph: it confirms the picture is the signed one, not that the holder is the person in it. Enhanced cards add that check, but without liveness detection — a good photo of the cardholder held up to the camera would pass — so it belongs at a staffed counter.
  • The face model was not trained on Maldivian faces and the threshold was calibrated on a public dataset of mostly Western faces. Error rates for the real population need measuring before the Enhanced tier is relied on.
  • Nothing in the payload is encrypted. Anyone who scans the card can read every field, exactly as they could by looking at it.

Built on

The signing approach follows oelna/signed-qr-codes, which demonstrates BLS-signed QR payloads; its Noble BLS, LZString and Nayuki QR modules are vendored unchanged. Reading QR codes uses jsQR. The image hash is a JavaScript port of the DCT hash from pHash.org.

No build step and no backend: every page is static and every check runs in the browser.
