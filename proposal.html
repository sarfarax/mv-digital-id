Making the National Identity Card Verifiable

A cryptographically signed QR code that binds the printed details and the cardholder’s photograph to the issuing authority — deployable with the next print run, verifiable offline on any phone.

Executive summary

The Maldivian national identity card is checked constantly — at banks, police checkpoints, ferry counters, clinics and workplaces — yet nothing on it can actually be verified. A teller, an officer or a clerk decides whether a card is genuine by looking at it. That is a judgement about printing quality, not a verification of fact, and it fails against any forger with a decent printer.

This proposal adds one element to the card: a QR code carrying the printed details plus a compact fingerprint of the cardholder’s photograph, all signed with the issuing authority’s cryptographic key. Any phone can then confirm, in about a tenth of a second and with no internet connection, that the details were issued by the Department of National Registration, that they have not been altered since, that the photograph on the card is the one that was signed, and that the card is still in date.

The change is deliberately small. Card stock, layout, issuance workflow and legal basis all stay as they are. No card is recalled: cards gain the QR as they are naturally reissued. Verification needs no reader, no app install, no account and no connectivity — it is a web page and a camera.

This complements rather than competes with the announced Smart ID card and with eFaas. A chip verifies nothing until readers are deployed nationwide, and eFaas requires enrolment, a smartphone and connectivity. This layer covers the case neither reaches: an in-person, offline check by anyone, anywhere, starting now. It fills the gap until 2027–2028, and it keeps earning its place afterwards as the fallback for the islands where readers and bandwidth arrive last.

The problem

The Department of National Registration has issued national identity cards since 1984 and serves a resident population the Maldives Bureau of Statistics projects at 590,079 for 2026, of whom 394,054 are Maldivian citizens.1 The card is mandatory for citizens under the Civil Registration and Issuance of Birth Certificates and National Identity Cards Act (Law No. 23/2022), and it is the document on which banking, travel, employment, land allocation, healthcare and voting all depend.

Every one of those transactions rests on a visual judgement. The person checking the card compares it against a remembered impression of what a real card looks like. There is no mechanism to establish that the name printed on a card is the name the state issued, or that the photograph is the one the state attached to that record.

This is not hypothetical

Maldivian courts treat document forgery as a serious offence under Chapter 15 of the Penal Code, and the judiciary has observed that such cases are increasing, with government institutions and the Maldives Police Service identifying them as a priority.2 In one MVR 8 million illegal recruitment and money laundering case, a search of the principal’s devices recovered digital copies of forged work permit documents, passport photographs of foreign workers and identity cards of Maldivians, alongside credentials used to access government administrative systems.3 Twelve people have separately been charged over forged registry documents used to claim land under the Binveriya housing scheme.4

A forged identity card is rarely the objective. It is the instrument used to reach something else: a permit, a bank account, a plot of land, a benefit. Making the card itself checkable removes the instrument.

The core weakness

Nothing binds the photograph to the data. Even if the printed text could be trusted, a genuine card with a substituted photograph would pass any inspection that does not include the original record. The photograph is the one element that connects a document to a human being, and it is the element with no protection at all.

The gap

Both flagship efforts are strong, and both leave the same case uncovered. eFaas is an online authentication system: it proves who you are to a website, given a smartphone, an enrolment and a working connection. The Smart ID card will carry a chip and a fingerprint, but a chip proves nothing until a reader is present, and readers must be procured, deployed, powered, maintained and integrated at every point where identity is checked.

Meanwhile the ordinary case — a person handing a plastic card across a counter to another person — remains a visual guess. It will remain a visual guess for the eighteen months to two years before Smart ID reaches scale, and it will remain one afterwards anywhere a reader has not been installed.

Where this wins

The government’s stated aim is to let citizens access services from any island and to eliminate costly inter-island travel. The islands that make that aim urgent are precisely the ones where chip readers and reliable bandwidth will arrive last. A check that needs only a camera and no network is strongest exactly where the alternatives are weakest.

The proposal

Add a QR code to the card. It contains the six printed fields, a 64-bit perceptual hash of the cardholder’s photograph, and a digital signature over all of it produced by the issuing authority’s private key.

MV2#A123456#Aishath Nasheeda Ibrahim#F#1991-04-17#2036-08-27#Ma. Blue Heaven, Male, Maldives#290d5eb82deaf087#<signature>

That is the entire change to the artefact. Nothing else about the card — its dimensions, stock, security printing, layout, the issuance process or the legal framework — needs to move.

Four things become checkable that are not checkable today:

  1. Authenticity. The signature verifies only against the authority’s published public key. A card produced by anyone else fails.
  2. Integrity. Altering any printed field breaks the signature, because every field is inside it.
  3. Photo binding. Replacing the photograph changes its perceptual hash, which no longer matches the signed one.
  4. Validity period. The expiry date is inside the signature, so it cannot be edited to extend a card’s life.

How it works

Digital signatures

The signed message is the version prefix, the six printed fields and the photo hash joined into one string. It is signed with BLS12-381, a pairing-friendly elliptic curve used in production by major blockchain and aggregate-signature systems. The signature is 96 bytes; the public key is 48 bytes.

The security property that matters is asymmetry. The Department of National Registration holds a private key and can produce signatures. Everyone else holds only the public key and can therefore check signatures but never create them. Publishing the public key nationally costs nothing and enables everyone; it grants no ability to issue.

Binding the photograph

A cryptographic hash cannot be used on a photograph, because a photograph that has been printed and re-photographed is never bit-identical to the original and a cryptographic hash would reject it. The card therefore carries a perceptual hash: an implementation of the DCT-based algorithm published by pHash.org, which reduces an image to 64 bits describing its low-frequency structure.

Two pictures of the same face produce nearly the same bits; two different faces do not. The comparison is a Hamming distance — a count of differing bits — against a configurable tolerance. Measured on the reference implementation:

0
bits differ, genuine card scanned flat
2
bits differ, genuine card photographed at an angle under noise
22
bits differ, substituted face
12
default acceptance threshold

The margin is wide in both directions: genuine captures sit far below the threshold even when degraded, and a different face sits far above it.

Finding the photograph without a scanner

The verifier must locate and normalise the portrait before hashing it. Because the card is a rigid layout with fixed geometry, and because QR decoders report the four corners of the symbol they found, those corners are enough. The verifier computes a homography from the QR corners, applies it to the known portrait rectangle, and lifts the photograph out with perspective and rotation removed — then normalises it to the same 300×400 greyscale raster the issuer hashed.

This is why a hand-held photograph of a card lying on a desk at an angle still verifies. The geometry is recovered mathematically rather than demanded of the user.

Fitting it on the card

The payload is compressed before encoding. A complete signed record is 302 characters, compressing to 266 bytes, which encodes as a version 10 QR symbol of 57 modules. In the 25 mm square allocated on the card that is 0.385 mm per module — comfortable for commercial card printing and well within the range of a modern phone camera.

Issuance

The DNR already operates a decentralised issuance network of 31 print centres and 183 collection centres, plus overseas missions.8 The proposal changes what those print centres print, not how they operate.

  1. Key ceremony. Generate the national signing key inside a hardware security module under documented multi-party control. The private key never leaves the HSM.
  2. Publish the public key. On a government domain, in the Gazette, and embedded in verifier applications. It is not secret.
  3. Integrate at the point of personalisation. When a card record is finalised, the system hashes the approved portrait, assembles the message, requests a signature from the HSM and renders the QR into the card template.
  4. Print as normal. The QR is ink. It adds no consumable, no chip, no encoding step and no per-card hardware.
  5. Reissue naturally. Cards gain the QR as they are renewed, replaced or newly issued. No recall is required, and the two populations coexist indefinitely.
Key custody is the whole ballgame

The security of every card ever issued reduces to the secrecy of one private key. It must live in certified hardware, with split control, audit logging and a rehearsed rotation procedure. The reference implementation ships a demonstration key in public source code precisely so that nobody mistakes it for a production system.

Verification

The verifier is a web page. It runs entirely in the browser: no backend call, no account, no data leaves the device. The sequence is:

  1. Locate and decode the QR in the camera frame, then decompress the payload.
  2. Check the signature against the trusted public key. If this fails, nothing else matters.
  3. Check the expiry date, which is inside the signature.
  4. Recover the portrait using the QR corners and the card’s fixed geometry.
  5. Re-hash it and compare with the signed hash.

Measured end to end on a desktop-class machine against the sample card:

102 ms

Measured in a Chromium browser on a twelve-core desktop against a 1370×864 card image. A mid-range phone should be expected to take several times longer; the operative point is that the result is immediate at human timescales, not that the specific figure transfers.

Deployment of the verifier

Because it is a static web page, distribution is trivial: a government URL, an optional installable progressive web app for offline use, or embedding inside existing apps such as eFaas or a bank’s. The public key can be pinned in the build so that an offline device still verifies correctly.

Security analysis

What this defeats

AttackOutcome
Wholly fabricated cardNo valid signature can be produced without the private key. Rejected.
Altering a name, address or date of birthEvery field is inside the signature. Rejected.
Replacing the photograph on a genuine cardSignature still valid; the perceptual hash no longer matches. Rejected at the portrait step.
Extending the validity of an expired cardExpiry is inside the signature. Rejected.
Copying a valid QR onto a different cardThe QR carries the identity and photo hash of its original holder, so the printed face and the signed data diverge. Rejected.
A card signed by a different authorityVerification is against one pinned public key. Rejected.

What this does not defeat

Stated plainly
  • No revocation offline. A signature says “this was issued”, never “this is still valid”. A card reported stolen the day after issue still verifies. Only an online status check closes this, and that necessarily requires connectivity.
  • It confirms the picture, not the person. The portrait check proves the photograph on the card is the signed one. It does not prove the bearer is the person in the photograph. That remains a human judgement, exactly as it is today, unless biometric matching is added.
  • Private key compromise is catastrophic. An attacker with the signing key can mint cards indistinguishable from genuine ones. This is the argument for an HSM and for a rehearsed key rotation plan.
  • Nothing is encrypted. Anyone who scans the card reads every field — exactly as they could by reading the card. Whether a home address belongs in a machine-readable code any passer-by can scan is a policy question, addressed in section 11.
  • A genuine card presented by a look-alike. Perceptual hashing tolerates change by construction, so a sufficiently similar face could fall inside the threshold. The tolerance is configurable, and this is the specific weakness that face-vector matching would address.

Extension: face vector matching

A future card tier can additionally carry a compact binary code derived from a face recognition embedding — a locality-sensitive hash, not a cryptographic one, compared by Hamming distance in the same way. Published results place 128-bit PCA-ITQ face templates at around 99% true-accept at 1% false-accept.9 Sixteen bytes fits in the existing QR budget with room to spare.

That upgrade is what would let a verifier compare the signed template against a live capture of the person standing there, turning a document check into an identity check. It is designed as an optional tier so that standard cards remain fully valid. It also raises materially larger privacy questions, since a biometric template is a linkable identifier in a way that a photo hash is not.

Coexistence with Smart ID

These are layers, not competitors, and the sensible end state runs both. The chip serves high-assurance and transactional use — payments, fingerprint verification, online authentication at institutions equipped to read it. The signed QR serves universal offline visual verification, on the same card, for everyone else. Printing a QR on the Smart ID card costs nothing and means the card still proves something when the reader is broken, absent or out of power.

Cost

The distinguishing economic feature is the absence of per-card marginal cost. A QR code is ink on a card that is already being printed.

ItemNature
Per-card costEffectively zero. No chip, no encoding hardware, no additional consumable.
Hardware security moduleOne-off procurement plus support. The single significant capital item.
Issuance integrationOne-off software integration with the existing personalisation workflow.
VerifierStatic hosting. No backend, no per-verification cost, no scaling cost.
Reader hardwareNone, at any verification point, ever.
Card replacement programmeNone. Cards gain the QR through the normal reissue cycle.

The cost profile is dominated by one-off integration rather than recurring or per-unit spend.

Governance and privacy

The Digital Identity Bill establishes a Digital Identity Technical Advisory Committee and sets out the duties of identity providers and the safeguards governing personal data.7 This proposal fits naturally within that framework and would benefit from being considered under it.

  • No new data is collected. The QR contains exactly what is already printed on the face of the card, plus a hash of the photograph that is already on the card.
  • No new database. Verification is offline and stateless. There is no central log of who checked whose card, and therefore no surveillance surface to govern.
  • The photo hash is not a photograph. Sixty-four bits cannot reconstruct an image; it can only be compared against a candidate.
  • Machine readability is the real question. The address is printed on the card today, but a QR makes it bulk-collectable by anyone with a camera. Options include omitting the address from the payload at some cost to verification coverage, or accepting it as equivalent to what is already visible. This is a policy decision, not a technical one.
  • The face tier would change this analysis. A biometric template is a linkable identifier and would warrant a full privacy impact assessment under ISO/IEC 24745 principles before adoption.

Risks

RiskMitigation
Signing key compromiseHSM with split control, audit logging, rehearsed rotation, and a schema that already carries a version prefix so a re-key can be distinguished.
Genuine cards rejected in poor conditionsThreshold calibration during the pilot against real printed stock; the tolerance is configurable and the signature check is unaffected by lighting.
QR damaged by wearError correction is built into the QR standard; the level can be raised at the cost of a denser symbol. Visual inspection remains available as a fallback.
Operators over-trusting a pass resultVerifier wording states what each result does and does not prove. Training must stress that a valid card says nothing about the bearer.
Perceived duplication with Smart IDPosition explicitly as the offline layer of the same programme, printed on the Smart ID card itself when that arrives.
Public confusion between card generationsBoth populations verify correctly; the verifier reports which generation it read. No flag day is required.

Recommendation

Authorise a time-boxed pilot. The engineering is complete and demonstrable: a reference implementation of issuance and verification exists, is fully tested, and is available for inspection. What it has not had is contact with real card stock, real cameras, real lighting and an adversarial security review. That is what a pilot buys, and it is inexpensive to buy.

Concretely, the decisions sought are:

  1. Approve a pilot with the Department of National Registration and the Maldives Digital Service.
  2. Commission an independent security review of the scheme and its reference implementation.
  3. Authorise HSM procurement and a documented key ceremony.
  4. Agree the policy position on which fields belong in a machine-readable payload.
  5. Direct that the Smart ID card specification reserve space for a printed signed QR, so the offline layer survives the transition.

The argument for acting now is simply that the alternative is to leave the card unverifiable for another two years while the chip programme matures. The cost of closing that gap is one integration and one HSM, and the capability does not become redundant when the chip arrives.

Payload format

Fields are joined with #, following the convention of the signed-QR-code scheme this implementation is based on:

MV2#<idNumber>#<name>#<sex>#<dob>#<expiry>#<address>#<photoHash>#<signature> \________________________ signed message ________________________/
  • Version prefix — identifies the field layout. Readers refuse versions they do not know rather than guessing at positional fields.
  • Six printed fields — ID number, name, sex, date of birth, expiry date and permanent address.
  • photoHash — 64-bit perceptual hash of the normalised portrait, 16 hexadecimal characters.
  • signature — BLS12-381 over everything preceding it, 96 bytes as 192 hexadecimal characters.

The complete string is compressed with LZString and encoded as a QR code in binary byte mode. A URL-safe text mode is also supported for readers that mangle raw bytes, at roughly a third more payload.
